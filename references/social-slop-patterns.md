# Social Media Slop Patterns

Platform-specific patterns that make social content obviously AI-generated. Detection rules and what to do instead.

## Cross-Platform Tells

These patterns appear on every platform and are the fastest way to spot AI-generated social content.

### Emoji as Structure
Using emojis as bullet points, paragraph separators, and emphasis markers. Rocket, lightbulb, fire, pointing finger, check mark — used not for expression but for formatting.
- **The tell**: 5+ emojis per post, used as structural elements rather than emotional punctuation
- **Fix**: Zero emojis is better than emoji spam. If you use emojis, max 1-2 per post, for genuine expression, not formatting.

### Manufactured Urgency
"You need to read this," "This changed everything," "Stop scrolling," "I can't believe more people aren't talking about this."
- **The tell**: The urgency doesn't match the content. The "revelation" is common knowledge dressed up.
- **Fix**: Let the content create its own urgency. If the insight is genuinely surprising, the reader will feel it without being told to.

### Generic Listicle Format
"5 reasons why..." "7 things I wish I knew..." "10 mistakes you're making..." AI defaults to numbered lists because they're structured and safe.
- **The tell**: The list could apply to any person in any industry. The items are obvious. The number feels arbitrary.
- **Fix**: If you do use lists, make every item specific to YOUR experience. "The mistake that cost us $40K in Q3" beats "Not optimizing your workflow."

### The Hook-Body Disconnect
An attention-grabbing hook followed by content that doesn't deliver on the promise. AI generates great hooks but can't back them up with substance.
- **The tell**: The first line promises insight; the body delivers platitudes.
- **Fix**: Write the body first. The hook should be the most interesting thing FROM the body, not a separate bait.

### Vague Advice That Names No Specifics
"Optimize your workflow," "Build meaningful relationships," "Invest in yourself." Advice so general it applies to everyone and therefore helps no one.
- **The tell**: No specific tool, dollar amount, person, failure, or timeline is mentioned.
- **Fix**: Name the thing. "We switched from Notion to Linear and cut our sprint planning from 2 hours to 25 minutes" is specific. "Optimize your project management" is slop.

## LinkedIn-Specific Patterns

### Broetry
Every sentence on its own line. Forces "see more" clicks (gaming the algorithm). Creates false dramatic pauses.
```
I got fired on a Tuesday.

I had $200 in my bank account.

No plan. No backup. No safety net.

But here's what I learned.
```
- **The tell**: Single sentences as paragraphs. False drama through formatting. The structure is the content.
- **Fix**: Write actual paragraphs. If the content doesn't hold up in paragraph form, the structure was doing all the work.

### "Agree?" / "Thoughts?" Closers
Manufactured engagement bait. Asks for interaction without providing enough substance to have a real opinion about.
- **The tell**: Generic question at the end that any post could use
- **Fix**: If you want engagement, make a specific claim people can actually disagree with. "React Native is dead for new projects in 2026" invites real discussion. "Agree?" does not.

### The Humble-Brag Arc
"3 years ago I was [low point]. Today I [achievement]." Fabricated or exaggerated rags-to-riches narratives.
- **The tell**: The "low point" is suspiciously cinematic. The timeline is suspiciously clean. No messy middle.
- **Fix**: If you have a real story, tell the messy middle. The part that doesn't fit the narrative is the part that's interesting.

### "Unpopular opinion:" + Popular Opinion
"Unpopular opinion: toxic managers are bad for retention." The framing of controversy around something everyone agrees with.
- **The tell**: The "unpopular opinion" would get 95%+ agreement from any audience
- **Fix**: If you have an actual unpopular opinion, just state it without the label. The disagreement in the comments will prove it's unpopular.

### The Motivational Quote Card
AI-generated face image + inspirational text overlay. Posted by accounts that do nothing but post these.
- **The tell**: The face is AI-generated (see image-slop-tells.md). The quote is generic. The account has no original content.

### Engagement Pod Patterns
Groups of accounts commenting "Great insight!" "So true!" "Love this!" within minutes of posting. AI-generated comments are often shorter, more generic, and arrive in clusters.
- **The tell**: Comments that could apply to literally any post. "This is gold" on a post about spreadsheets.

## Instagram-Specific Patterns

### Template Carousels
Same design template used across hundreds of accounts. Gradient backgrounds, centered text, swipe-for-more format. All looking identical.
- **The tell**: The design could be any brand. No custom typography, color, or illustration.
- **Fix**: If you do carousels, make the design distinctly yours. Custom illustrations, brand-specific color palette, your actual screenshots or photos.

### Robotic Voiceover Reels
TTS (text-to-speech) narration over stock footage or AI-generated visuals. The voice is a tell — even good TTS has a rhythm that humans detect.
- **The tell**: Uniform pacing. No breathing pauses. No verbal tics. Perfect pronunciation.
- **Fix**: Use your own voice. Imperfect delivery > perfect robot. If you must use TTS, edit the timing to add pauses and variation.

### Engagement Bait Reels
"Save this for later!" "Share with someone who needs this!" "Comment YES if you agree!" Direct instructions to boost algorithm metrics.
- **The tell**: The instruction is more prominent than the content.
- **Fix**: Create content worth saving/sharing. The action follows naturally.

## X/Twitter-Specific Patterns

### The Thread Formula
"I studied [impressive thing] for [impressive time]. Here are [number] things most people miss: (thread)" followed by a generic listicle.
- **The tell**: The "study" is never cited. The findings are common knowledge. The thread could have been a single tweet.
- **Fix**: If you have real findings, lead with the most surprising one. "Most people think X but the data shows Y. Here's the data: [link]."

### Ratio-Bait Takes
Deliberately provocative statements designed to generate quote tweets and replies. Not genuine opinion — manufactured controversy.
- **The tell**: The take doesn't match the account's usual content. It's positioned to be wrong enough to provoke but not so wrong it's ignored.
- **Fix**: Have genuine opinions. The best engagement comes from being consistently interesting, not occasionally outrageous.

## TikTok-Specific Patterns

### AI Face Filter + Narration
AI-generated avatar lip-syncing to AI-generated script. Zero human involvement in any layer.
- **The tell**: Perfect lip sync with no natural variation. No environmental sounds. Background doesn't move.

### The "[Topic] They Don't Want You to Know" Format
Conspiracy framing around mundane information. AI generates these because the format is highly structured and easily templated.
- **The tell**: "They" is never identified. The "secret" information is publicly available.
- **Fix**: If you have genuinely non-obvious information, explain WHO benefits from it not being known and provide your source.

## What Good Social Content Looks Like

1. **Specific over general.** Name the tool, the number, the person, the failure. Specifics are human.
2. **Your voice, not A voice.** Write how you actually talk. Record yourself explaining it verbally and transcribe — that's your voice.
3. **Show the work.** Screenshots, recordings, photos of the actual process. Not polish, proof.
4. **One idea per post.** AI generates comprehensive overviews. Humans have one thing they can't stop thinking about.
5. **Respond to real things.** React to specific events, conversations, or experiences. AI can't reference what happened yesterday in your Slack.
6. **Imperfect delivery.** A slightly shaky selfie video beats a polished AI-narrated reel for trust.
